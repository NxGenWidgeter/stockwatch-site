Stockwatch is an inventory dashboard for anyone who counts stock and hates running out: a clinic, a pharmacy, a workshop, a shop, a garage, a kitchen. It watches how fast each item actually moves, works out how many days of cover you have left, and puts the reorder decision in front of you before the shelf is empty. It runs on one machine you already own — no cloud account, no per-seat pricing, no data leaving the building.

How it works

Four steps, and the fourth one is the point.

Most inventory tools stop at a table of numbers. Stockwatch's job is the decision you make on Monday morning, so the arithmetic runs on your own history rather than a guess.

  1. Bring the list in

    Import your spreadsheet — CSV or XLSX, straight from Excel, custom columns and all. Every distinct supplier name becomes a record you can give a lead time to.

  2. Count what's on the shelf

    Type counts in the dashboard, or let the steppers do it at the shelf. Each change is a timestamped movement — that log is what everything else is built from.

  3. Let it read your usage

    Daily rate over a rolling window, days of cover, and a spike detector that compares the latest day against the 14-day baseline. Items with no count yet are marked unknown, not zero.

  4. Order, schedule, receive

    Schedule the delivery with an amount and an estimate in days. It shows as +200 in 7d until it arrives — then one click adds it to stock and closes the loop.

It alerts you, so you don't have to look

Every 15 minutes (configurable) it evaluates the whole catalogue and sends one digest of everything below its reorder point — by email, by webhook into Slack or Teams, and always into the in-app feed. No notification storm: one message per run, with the exact numbers and the channel it used.

People, with limits that fit a business

Owner runs accounts and settings. Team leader manages stock, inventory and their own team. Member moves stock and nothing else. Guest reads. Access is enforced on every request, not just hidden in the interface — and sign-ups ship switched off.

Exports that survive an accountant

CSV and XLSX round-trip cleanly back into the app; the PDF is a one-page report. The exports honour whatever filter and sort you're looking at.

What the catalogue costs

Usage priced at each item's own unit price, for a month or a year, with a per-category breakdown. Usage is read from the movement log once an item has been drawn down on three or more separate days; otherwise it falls back to that item's yearly quantity. Stock on hand is never treated as a usage figure — one stock-take would otherwise read as a month of runaway spending.

A budget with two ends, and a counter you keep by hand

Set a min–max budget per month or per year, in whatever currency you keep books in (RM by default). Balance after shows what's left at both ends, and a coverage calculator says how long the budget lasts at the current burn rate. The yearly spend figure moves three ways — Add a payment, Set the exact number your accounts show, or Reset for a new year — each move stamped with the date and the account that made it. Touch it in one year and read it in the next and it says stale instead of rolling over.

Candles, when a line hides too much

Line | Candles in the chart header. Each candle is one period of the window you picked: open and close are the level at its start and end, the wick is how far it actually reached. A delivery rises green, consumption falls red, and movement-free periods are dimmed rather than drawn flat.

Rename a category once

Settings → Categories lists every category with its item count. Renaming moves every item in it in a single write; rename onto a name that already exists and the two merge, with the toast telling you how many items moved. A category is still a string on each row, so a typo in an import is one rename from fixed instead of sixty edits.

A danger zone that argues with you

Owner only, two scopes: Clear inventory wipes stock, history and suppliers but keeps accounts, teams and settings; Factory reset takes the whole thing back to the first-run screen. Three things have to be right before anything is removed — your password re-entered, the word DELETE typed out, and a backup ticked, which writes a copy of the database first. A wrong password answers 403, so a dialog doesn't bounce you to the sign-in page and lose its place.

Hardened where it counts

Passwords go through 600,000 PBKDF2-SHA256 rounds and are rehashed on the next sign-in after a policy bump, with no forced resets. Ten characters minimum, the usual breach-list guesses and your own username refused. Responses carry a Content-Security-Policy and a Permissions-Policy; HSTS appears only when TLS is on. A state-changing request arriving from another site is refused before it reaches a route, and the database file is 0600 from the moment it is created.

Before you hand it to anyone

  • Backups leave the machine. A nightly job and a restore script ship with it; a backup on the same disk protects you from a bad upgrade, not from a dead disk.
  • One hostname, one certificate. Behind TLS, turn on cookie_secure so the session cookie is marked Secure. Behind plain HTTP, leave it off.
  • A health probe. /api/health answers without a session, so an uptime monitor can watch it from somewhere else.
  • The owner's own password. Not a default, not one you know.

Power & outages

A plain surge protector protects the hardware.
It does nothing for the outage.

Plenty of small installations buy a power strip, feel prepared, and then discover the real failure mode: the box is fine, but it's off, and nobody is in the building to press the button. This is the part worth spending an afternoon on before go-live.

Surge protector only

Mains→ Strip→ Box→ Dashboard

Rides out nothing. A spike is absorbed; a two-second dip reboots the machine, and a hard power cut mid-write is exactly the case SQLite's journal is there for — but the dashboard is still gone until someone notices.

UPS + auto-restart

Mains→ UPS→ Box→ Dashboard

A small 600 VA unit keeps a 60 MB process and a monitor-less mini-PC alive for far longer than a brownout lasts, and it rides out the flicker that reboots a bare strip. Add Restart=on-failure and enabling at boot, and the box comes back on its own after a real cut.

The outage checklist

  • UPS on the box, not on the desk lamp. One outlet, sized to the machine — a 600 VA unit is generous for this workload.
  • Service enabled at boot with Restart=on-failure. That is the difference between "it came back" and "it needs a person".
  • Nightly backup to a different machine. The database is one file; a restore is a copy and a restart, not a project.
  • An uptime probe from outside hitting /api/health, so you learn about the outage before a branch does.
  • A named person who can reboot it at 9pm. Decide that before selling, because you will be making that call.

What it does not have

There is no high availability: one box, one database file, one writer. That is a deliberate trade for running on hardware a clinic can afford. If the box is down, the dashboard is down — so the UPS, the backup and the reboot plan are not extras, they are the availability story.

What survives a cut

SQLite in WAL mode with a per-write transaction: a power loss mid-write rolls back to the last committed change, and the movement log is the same journal. Expect to lose at most the single action that was in flight — not the day's counts.

Branch accounts

One account per branch, named so the audit log reads like a map.

Stockwatch uses an email-shaped identifier to sign in, and prints it next to every change it records. That makes the naming scheme an operational decision, not a cosmetic one: pick it before the first branch is created and the movement log stays readable for years.

A scheme that sorts

{branch}{sequence}@{your-domain}

yourcoKL001@yourco.my      # Kuala Lumpur, first account
yourcoPERAK001@yourco.my   # Perak
yourcoPERAK002@yourco.my   # Perak, second person
yourcoJH001@yourco.my      # Johor

Use a domain you own

@email.com is not a domain anyone can register — the examples above use your own domain instead. It matters for a practical reason: password-reset links and alert digests are delivered to that address, so it has to be a mailbox that exists. If you don't want a mailbox per branch, a catch-all or plus-addressing (it+kl001@…) on one domain works, as long as the address stays identical everywhere it's typed.

How the roles line up

  • Owner — the company, not a branch. This is the only account that can create users and teams, so it should be a head-office address, not the busiest branch.
  • Team leader — one per branch. Runs that branch's stock and adds or removes its members. Cannot create accounts.
  • Member — the staff at the shelf. Stock movements only.
  • Guest — an auditor or locum who needs to read and nothing else.

One team per branch

Create a team per branch with that branch's manager as its leader. Grouping and the activity view then read per branch without a custom column, and offboarding is one action: remove the person from the team, then disable the account.

What an address buys you

The activity feed and every movement row carry the account that made them. With a branch code in the address, "who cut 400 tubes on Tuesday" is answerable from the log without a lookup table.

Honest limits

What it doesn't do, so nobody finds out later.

No lot or expiry tracking

Nothing in the model knows a unit can spoil. If expiry dates matter — and in a clinic or a pharmacy they usually do — that is a feature to add, not a setting. Until then, keep the lot and expiry on the box.

Day boundaries are UTC

Usage buckets and spike windows roll over on UTC days, so a branch eight hours ahead sees "today" start at 08:00 local. Consistent, but worth knowing.

One lead time per supplier

A vendor that ships some lines in three days and others in thirty needs either a per-item override or two supplier records.

One writer

SQLite and a single process: comfortable for tens of users and thousands of items, not a platform for hundreds of concurrent writers.

Exports exclude incoming stock

CSV, XLSX and PDF show what is on the shelf. Scheduled deliveries live in the dashboard until they are received.

Costs are an estimate, not a ledger

The cost panel multiplies measured usage by each item's current unit price, so a price change rewrites history and an item with no price is counted as unpriced rather than guessed at. The yearly spend figure is typed in by a person. Treat both as a steer on spending, not as something to reconcile your accounts against.

You are the update channel

No auto-update, no telemetry, no phone-home. Upgrades are a tarball and a restart, done deliberately.
