Self-hosted inventory · one machine · your data stays in the building

Stock that tells you
what to order.

Stockwatch is an inventory dashboard for anyone who counts stock and hates running out: a clinic, a pharmacy, a workshop, a shop, a lab, a kitchen, a store room. It records every movement in and out, learns how fast each item actually goes, turns that into days of cover, keeps what is on order apart from what is on the shelf, prices what you use against a budget — and puts the reorder decision in front of you before the shelf is empty. It runs on one machine you already own: no cloud account, no per-seat subscription, nothing leaving the building.

The whole product, no gaps

Everything it does,
in the order it matters.

Everything below is in the one build. No add-ons, no modules to unlock, no API key: the same software runs your shelf whether you count three hundred items or thirty thousand.

Movements

Every change is a row

Stock in, stock out, a count, an import, a delivery marked arrived — each writes a movement with who, when and why. Changes are atomic, so two people pressing −1 at the same moment cannot lose a count.

Cover

Days of cover, not just a number

Every row shows stock ÷ daily use and turns amber when it is inside the lead-time window. “Nine days left” is a decision; “three thousand units” is not.

Reorder

Reorder points you can defend

Fill missing reorder points sets each one from its own usage: daily rate × (lead time + safety days). A number you type by hand always wins, and an item with no threshold never fires a false alarm.

On order

Ordered and arrived are two numbers

Scheduled stock sits beside the shelf figure as +24 · in 5d, amber once it slips. You enter the wait in days, the board shows the date, and only mark arrived adds it to stock — through the movement log.

Suppliers

Lead times live on the vendor

Every distinct vendor in your workbook becomes a supplier record. Set the delivery days once and every item from that vendor inherits them; anything without one falls back to your default.

Attention

Low, out, healthy — one click

Per-item thresholds, filter chips with live counts, red rows, an OUT badge at zero, and a sort that puts the worst first. An imported item with no stock column reads no count, never a fake zero.

Anomalies

Usage spikes found for you

The latest active day is compared with the 14-day average: a spike must clear it by 75% and mean + 2σ, on at least three units of normal use. Flagged on the row, marked on the chart, and sent in the same digest as low stock.

History

A stock chart worth reading

Click a row and the panel plots its level over 7, 30 or 90 days with the reorder line, a hover read-out of each movement and a marker where a spike was detected. Every row also carries a 30-day sparkline, so the table answers "trending down?" without a click.

Money

What it costs to use

Usage is priced at each item's unit price and totalled for the month, so the restock panel can say “usage this month ≈ RM 783 · this order ≈ RM 50”. One figure, next to the decision it belongs to — no budget spreadsheet to keep in step.

Housekeeping
Income

What actually earns

Selling is a button on the row: quantity, price, done — stock comes out through the movement log and the cash goes on the record. Each sale keeps the cost price it had at that moment, so profit is real profit, and the header names the most profitable item and the least for the last thirty days.

Categories renamed once

A category is a string on each row, so renaming is one action for all sixty items — and renaming onto a name that already exists merges the two and tells you how many moved.

Your workbook

Columns you didn't configure

Any header the importer does not recognise becomes a custom column (text or number) instead of being dropped. Values survive export → edit → re-import and go with the column when you delete it.

Working the list

Group, subtotal, bulk edit

Group by category, supplier, status or a custom column; each group carries its own item, unit and value subtotal and its checkbox selects the lot. Set category, supplier, reorder point or cover days across every ticked row at once — or delete them.

Import

Their sheet, not ours

.xlsx and .csv, matched by header name (Item Code, Description, Qty, Reorder Point, Vendor…), a title row tolerated, thousands separators and currency symbols cleaned, rows upserted on SKU. No openpyxl needed on the server.

Export

CSV, Excel, and a PDF

All three honour the current filter, so Low + PDF is a one-page order list with totals and your own columns. Excel is written by hand, so it opens in Excel and WPS without a repair prompt.

Alerts

Email, webhook, or the feed

A background check runs every 15 minutes (yours to change) and sends one digest per run: SMTP email to any number of recipients, a JSON POST for Slack or Teams, and always the in-app feed with the channels it actually used. With SMTP off, the digest is written to data/outbox/*.eml so you can read it before going live.

People

Four roles, real ones

Owner, leader (of a team), member and guest — read-only, no stepper, no checkbox. Owners create accounts and teams; a leader adds their own people to their own team. Sign-up is off by default, an allowlist can gate it further, and sessions can be cut from the users panel.

Record

An audit trail that answers “who”

Every write is logged with the account that made it: who took the stock out, who edited the price, who deleted the line, who cleared the whole inventory. The feed is in the dashboard and on the API.

Safety net

Backup and a tested restore

One SQLite file holds everything. The shipped scripts take a consistent snapshot of a running instance and verify a restore; a nightly timer keeps 30 of them on this machine. The database is owner-only on disk.

Security

Local only, and it means it

The launcher refuses any address but loopback and the app answers 403 to anything that did not arrive over it — so a hand-started server still serves nothing. PBKDF2-SHA256 at 600,000 rounds, rehashed on login; CSP, CSRF checks and no-store headers on every response.

Finishing touches

Five looks, installable, printable

Dark, light, gold, parchment or follow-the-system, applied before first paint and remembered per browser. It installs to the desktop from the browser, and every list can be exported or printed.

Practical use

What a week actually looks like.

Not a feature tour — six moments where the board pays for itself. This is the loop a stock controller runs with Stockwatch on one screen.

  1. Monday, 08:10

    The order run

    Filter to Low, sort worst first, and read the cover column: nine days, four days, two days. Tick the rows you are ordering, Export PDF and the one-page list goes to the supplier with quantities and totals on it — no spreadsheet, no retyping.

  2. Monday, 08:25

    What is already coming

    Before ordering, the green pills say what is on the way and when. Add the new deliveries in days (the supplier's lead time fills the field), and the item now shows shelf + on order as two separate numbers. Nothing arrives early by accident.

  3. Wednesday, 11:00

    A delivery lands

    Open the item, press arrived on that delivery. Stock goes up, a movement row records who did it and that it came from a scheduled order, cover recalculates, and the green pill disappears. If it was late, it was amber before you clicked.

  4. Friday, 16:00

    The count

    Type the counted figure into the stepper; each change is a movement like any other, so the variance is explainable and the usage graph keeps its shape. A shelf that has been quietly walked off with shows up as a spike on the row and the chart.

  5. Month end

    The number for the meeting

    Under the list, one line prices the month's usage and the order you are about to place — enough to sanity-check a delivery against a budget without turning the board into an accounting package.

  6. When something changes

    A person leaves, a branch opens

    Cut their session and their account; the audit trail keeps what they did. A new branch is a team and an account named for it (perak001@yourcompany.my), so every movement and every alert says which shelf it came from.

Set up

One machine you already own, and about ten minutes.

No server, no Docker, no database to install. The Windows build is an installer; the Linux and macOS build is one archive with systemd or launchd units inside.

  1. Pick the machine

    Any PC that is on during working hours — the counter PC, the office desktop, a small mini-PC in the store. 2 GB of RAM is plenty for a catalogue of thousands of items.

  2. Install and open

    Windows: run the installer, the dashboard opens at localhost:8123 and starts with the session. Linux/macOS: unpack and run run.sh, or install the unit so it comes back after a reboot.

  3. Make the owner account

    First run asks for an email and a password (10 characters minimum). Sign-up stays off, so nobody else can create an account on their own.

  4. Bring your list in

    Import the workbook you already have. Headers are matched by name, unknown ones become your columns, and items with no stock column land as no count rather than a fake zero.

  5. Set suppliers, then let it run

    Give each vendor its delivery days, press fill missing reorder points, and the board starts telling you what needs ordering the same afternoon.

tar -xzf stockwatch-1.7.1.tar.gz && cd stockwatch-1.7.1
./run.sh                      # creates its venv, opens :8123
# optional: run it as a service
sudo cp deploy/stockwatch.service /etc/systemd/system/
sudo systemctl enable --now stockwatch

curl -s localhost:8123/api/health   # {"ok":true,"version":"1.7.1"}

What it needs

Python 3.11 or newer on Linux/macOS (the Windows build ships its own runtime), 60 MB of memory, one SQLite file in data/. No internet connection at run time — the update check is you, unzipping a newer archive.

Honest limits

What it doesn't do, so nobody finds out later.

It runs on one machine

By design: it binds loopback only and refuses anything else. Other people use it on that machine, or on its screen — not from their own laptop over the network.

No TLS, no MFA, no SSO

The transport is your desk, not the internet. Login is email + password with a lockout after five failures, and sessions you can cut. There is no encryption at rest.

One database per install

No multi-tenancy: a second branch is a team and an account, not a separate tenant. Separate shelves that never mix need their own install.

It is not an accounting system

It prices usage from unit price, not from invoices. The yearly spend is typed in from your accounts — the app will not reconcile a purchase ledger.

Stock is a number

No barcodes, no lot numbers, no expiry dates, no serials. If you need batch traceability this is the wrong tool and I will say so.

Backups live on the same disk

The nightly copy protects against a bad upgrade or a corrupt file, not against the disk dying. Copy the folder somewhere else weekly — it is one file.
